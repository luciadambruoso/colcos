Action Agenda

The future we want

Our action reflection is collectively developed to represent moving towards a desired goal via a series of interconnected steps and events. We reviewed alternative courses of action and specified and appraised interventions, their feasibility and acceptability, and potential barriers including cultural factors.

Three priorities were agreed with the health authority.

01

Targeting root causes

Cessation support has to recognise that smoking is, for some people, the only stress relief they have. Healthy alternatives to de-stress were identified: CBT, meditation, understanding your own mind.

02

Inclusive access

Visibility of campaigns. There was frustration in the room: how can people access a service if they do not know it exists?

03

Incentivising

Money saved for a holiday rather than for groceries or energy. ‘Free’ matters enormously for people who are struggling.

Appraising the campaigns

Community partners went through the existing cessation materials themselves — the wording, the images, where they were placed. The campaigns framed the money saved from quitting as survival: it would cover the fuel bill, or the groceries. Partners wanted the opposite. They wanted aspiration — a holiday, something to move towards — because survival framing describes the trap rather than the way out of it. They wanted ‘free’ said far more loudly, since many did not know the service cost nothing. And they wanted the stress underneath the smoking treated as the target, with healthy alternatives such as mindfulness and meditation offered alongside cessation.

On packaging

Packaging came up alongside the campaigns. Graphic health warnings on cigarette packets are among the measures credited with driving smoking down in Scotland, but partners raised the question of whether they still land — whether long exposure has produced desensitisation to the images. The same appraisal ran the other way for e-cigarettes, which partners described as packaged to appeal: bright, gift-like, and attractive to young people. One of the photovoice plates is titled Rainbow of Toxins for exactly that reason.

Still to comeThis is drawn from a short note in the roadshow deck reading ‘(No)smoking sensations? De-sensitisation’ under the shared action agenda, together with the photovoice captions. I have read it as desensitisation to the graphic warnings — worth confirming that is what was meant before it goes live.

The message and the messenger

The appraisal opened onto something larger than marketing. Discussing ‘the message and the messenger’, partners and practitioners arrived at a finding that none of the workshops had set out to look for: cessation support is largely absent from the places where people already come for help with addiction. Alcohol and drug services hold exactly the relationships that cessation needs, and the peer support running inside them is the trusted route in.

It surfaced because the same people kept meeting — community partners, third sector practitioners and health improvement staff, repeatedly, in the same rooms. The health improvement team committed to explore embedding cessation services in peer support for alcohol and drugs, and to keep working with the group on future campaigns. It is the clearest example in the study of what dialogue produced that a survey would not have.

Mental health came through the process more powerfully than anyone had planned for. It is the reason the follow-on discussion turned towards a shared approach to public mental wellbeing across Grampian, and towards peer-led training with the mental health team.
