Assessment

The components of the approach

Cooperative action learning is often described as a philosophy. It is more useful to treat it as an assembly of separable parts, each doing a specific job at a specific cost. Anyone considering adopting it is really deciding which six things they can resource.

Component 01

Convening

Nine community members in under-served areas of rural Aberdeenshire took part as co-researchers rather than as subjects, recruited through Turning Point Scotland.

What it does

It settles the agenda before the researchers arrive, so the topics analysed are the ones the community named.

What it costs

It cannot be done cold. It depends entirely on an organisation with existing standing in the community, and that relationship takes years rather than months to build. This is the component most often skipped and least often substitutable.

Component 02

Community-generated evidence

Problem trees, stakeholder mapping and photovoice, produced and captioned by community researchers.

What it does

It produces categories the routine data has no field for — smoking as a response to financial stress, the normalisation of vaping, services that exist on paper but are inaccessible in practice.

What it costs

Nine people is not a sample and was never meant to be one. The claim these methods make is to validity, not to generalisability, and that distinction has to be held clearly or the work is easy to dismiss.

Component 03

A statistical counterpart

Secondary analysis of the Scottish Health Survey and national cessation data, set alongside what the community produced.

What it does

It is the component that stops participatory evidence being read as anecdote. It also produced the study’s sharpest finding — that quit attempts succeed at broadly similar rates across deprivation quintiles, while far fewer people in the most deprived quintile make one.

What it costs

It needs a data scientist on the team from the start, not brought in at the end to validate.

Component 04

Rapid reviews on community-set questions

Three briefs: on hypnosis, on e-cigarettes, and on how to talk about poverty.

What it does

It inverts who sets the review question. The community asked about hypnosis; the researchers would not have.

What it costs

Very little, which is why this is the most portable component of the whole approach. Any team with library access could run it next month.

Component 05

Joint analysis in learning spaces

Three further workshops in which service users and providers interpreted the same evidence together.

What it does

This is where the approach either works or does not. Analysis done jointly produces conclusions both parties are committed to; analysis done separately produces a report.

What it costs

It requires officials with the authority to change something to actually attend, repeatedly. That is a scheduling problem in appearance and a power problem in substance, and it is the component most likely to fail quietly.

Component 06

An action agenda, and returning to it

A shared agenda agreed with the health authority, followed in February 2024 by a return visit to review what had changed.

What it does

The agenda converts the preceding five components into something a health authority can act on. The return visit is what distinguishes participation from consultation — community partners saw their own intelligence in NHS Grampian’s new campaigns and said so.

What it costs

The return visit is unfunded in most study designs, and is almost always the first thing dropped.

Components 4 and 2 are cheap and transferable. Component 3 is a staffing decision. Components 1, 5 and 6 are the difficult ones — they depend on relationships and on institutional willingness rather than on method. A team adopting this approach without them would be running the technique without the conditions that make it work.

Podcast

Recovery Stories

The participatory research in this study was grounded in recovery spaces — peer support groups for alcohol, drugs and addiction run by Turning Point Scotland in Aberdeenshire. Those rooms were where the work could happen at all: places where people already had practice at being honest with each other, and where trust did not have to be built from nothing.

So peer support is not a footnote to this study. It is the condition that made the method possible, which is why we documented it in its own right. It is also what community partners marked as missing when they mapped who was available to help them stop smoking — peer support for smoking did not exist in the way it existed for addiction.

Four episodes, in which practitioners and peer support workers describe using their own experience to support recovery through connection and compassion. Hosted by Lucia D’Ambruoso, Aberdeen Centre for Health Data Science.
