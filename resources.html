Assessment

The components of the approach

Cooperative action learning is often described as a philosophy. It is more useful to treat it as an assembly of separable components, each of which does a specific job and carries a specific cost. Anyone considering adopting the approach is really deciding which of these six they can resource.

1. Convening

Nine community members in under-served areas of rural Aberdeenshire took part as co-researchers rather than as subjects, recruited through Turning Point Scotland. What it does: it settles the agenda before the researchers arrive, so the topics analysed are the ones the community named. What it costs: it cannot be done cold. It depends entirely on an organisation with existing standing in the community, and that relationship takes years rather than months to build. This is the component most often skipped and least often substitutable.

2. Community-generated evidence

Problem trees, stakeholder mapping and photovoice, produced and captioned by community researchers. What it does: it produces categories the routine data has no field for — smoking as a response to financial stress, the normalisation of vaping, services that exist on paper but are inaccessible in practice. What it costs: nine people is not a sample and was never meant to be one. The claim these methods make is to validity, not to generalisability, and that distinction has to be held clearly or the work is easy to dismiss.

3. A statistical counterpart

Secondary analysis of the Scottish Health Survey and national cessation data, set alongside what the community produced. What it does: it is the component that stops participatory evidence being read as anecdote. It also produced the study’s sharpest finding — that quit attempts succeed at broadly similar rates across deprivation quintiles, while far fewer people in the most deprived quintile make one. That reframes the problem from motivation to access. What it costs: it needs a data scientist on the team from the start, not brought in at the end to validate.

4. Rapid reviews on community-set questions

Three briefs, on hypnosis, on e-cigarettes, and on how to talk about poverty. What it does: it inverts who sets the review question. The community asked about hypnosis; the researchers would not have. What it costs: very little, which is why this is the most portable component of the whole approach. Any team with library access could run it next month.

5. Joint analysis in learning spaces

Three further workshops in which service users and providers interpreted the same evidence together. What it does: this is where the approach either works or does not. Analysis done jointly produces conclusions both parties are committed to; analysis done separately produces a report. What it costs: it requires officials with the authority to change something to actually attend, repeatedly. That is a scheduling problem in appearance and a power problem in substance, and it is the component most likely to fail quietly.

6. An action agenda, and returning to it

A shared agenda agreed with the health authority, followed in February 2024 by a return visit to review what had changed. What it does: the agenda converts the preceding five components into something a health authority can act on. The return visit is what distinguishes participation from consultation — community partners saw their own intelligence in NHS Grampian’s new campaigns and said so. What it costs: the return visit is unfunded in most study designs, and is almost always the first thing dropped.

The honest summary is that components 4 and 2 are cheap and transferable, component 3 is a staffing decision, and components 1, 5 and 6 are the difficult ones — they depend on relationships and on institutional willingness rather than on method. A team adopting this approach without them would be running the technique without the conditions that make it work.

Publication

The study, peer reviewed and open access

Cost of Living/Cost of Smoking: A Demonstration Study of Cooperative Action Learning to Understand and Address Smoking in Deprived Communities Within the Cost-of-Living Crisis

D’Ambruoso L, Stephen A, Cowan E, Innocent W, Marathia E, Littlejohn C, Duffy S, and the Cost of Living/Cost of Smoking Learning Partnership

Social Policy & Administration, 2024. doi:10.1111/spol.13082

Nine community-based participants took part in eight workshops as co-researchers, collecting and arranging new data and evidence. Three further workshops with service providers analysed and interpreted that data, appraised local action, and reflected on the process.

Podcast

Recovery Stories

Recovery Stories: peer support for connection and compassion enabling recovery from alcohol and drugs

A four-part podcast series sharing community empowerment experiences from peer supporters working with Turning Point Scotland in Aberdeenshire, helping communities dealing with alcohol and drug problems. Practitioners and peer support workers describe how they have used their own experience to support recovery from addiction through connection and compassion.

Peer support is the process of giving and receiving non-professional, non-clinical assistance from people with similar conditions or circumstances. It matters here because when community partners in this study mapped who was available to help them stop smoking, peer support groups were one of the things they marked as missing. The series is what that gap sounds like when it is filled.

Hosted by Lucia D’Ambruoso, Aberdeen Centre for Health Data Science, University of Aberdeen.
